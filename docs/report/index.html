Abstract

While legislative Question Time is universally acknowledged as a premier constitutional mechanism for compelling executive information, empirical scholarship has predominantly focused on national parliaments or treated the *asking* of questions as the sole proxy for accountability. Drawing upon the complete digital legislative record of the Meghalaya Legislative Assembly (2021–2026)—encompassing 2,217 questions, 11 sessions, and optical character recognition (OCR) extraction of 2,186 signed ministerial replies (98.6% corpus coverage)—this study introduces an auditable, multi-dimensional framework to measure the Preparedness Gap: the observable distance between legislative scrutiny and executive responsiveness. Rather than collapsing institutional performance into unweighted indices, we evaluate seven discrete vectors: unanswered demand, boilerplate vagueness, unsubstantiated assurance, internal inconsistency, evidentiary thinness, delivery pipeline attrition, and longitudinal non-resolution. We uncover three systemic pathologies: (1) Hyper-concentrated scrutiny, where three departments (Education, Health, PWD Roads) absorb 28.2% of questions and the opposition drives 79.7% of all inquiries, while budget-heavy fiscal ministries operate in near-total obscurity; (2) The Substance-Specificity Paradox, wherein 40.5% of ministerial replies are detailed in text length, but only 2.0% commit to a binding completion timeline, 3.4% designate an accountable officer, and 124 replies (5.7%) exhibit direct internal contradictions within a single paragraph; and (3) The Accountability Trap, wherein 62.1% of recurring subject families (41 of 66) remain stalled across multiple sessions without administrative progress language. These findings demonstrate that high formal procedural compliance (rapid 7-day median turnaround) can comfortably coexist with profound substantive information asymmetry. We conclude with five institutional reforms to transition subnational legislatures from ritualised information extraction to enforceable accountability.

Keywords: Legislative Oversight, Question Time, Executive Accountability, Information Asymmetry, Preparedness Gap, Subnational Governance, Indian Federalism, Bureaucratic Evasion.

Pillar · Theoretical Foundation

1. Theoretical Framework: Information Asymmetry and the Preparedness Gap

In parliamentary democracies, the Question Hour is celebrated as the constitutional zenith of executive accountability. The classical principal-agent model posits that elected legislators (principals) utilize parliamentary questions to overcome informational asymmetries, monitor bureaucratic execution, and discipline ministers (agents) (Strøm 2000; Pelizzo & Stapenhurst 2012). However, empirical reality—particularly in subnational legislatures in federal nations—frequently diverges from this normative ideal.

The Core Analytical Paradox: Does Question Time function as an effective instrument of substantive oversight, or does it degenerate into what Roycroft (2018) terms 'Show Time'—a ritualized performance where politicians extract political spectacle while departments deploy bureaucratic deflection to preserve informational monopolies?

Recent advances in legislative studies emphasize that oversight quality resides in the content of the ministerial reply, not merely in the volume of questions raised (Maricut-Akbik 2021; Akbik & Migliorati 2024). When executives face questioning, they possess a strategic choice: they can respond with substantive evidentiary transparency (specific figures, milestones, named contractors, verifiable timelines) or with defensive opacity (boilerplate rhetoric, procedural delays, dependency shifting, and non-committal promises).

1.1 Conceptualizing and Operationalizing 'The Preparedness Gap'

1.2 Hypotheses for Empirical Verification

Guided by legislative theory and the institutional realities of India's subnational assemblies (Gulzar & Pasquale 2017; Ojha & Mishra 2010), we formulate five testable empirical hypotheses:

  • H1 (Attention Concentration): Legislative scrutiny concentrates disproportionately on visible physical infrastructure and social services (Education, Health, Roads), neglecting macro-fiscal, regulatory, and institutional departments.
  • H2 (The Partisan Asymmetry Hypothesis): Legislative oversight is monopolized by opposition legislators, with ruling party backbenchers exhibiting near-zero scrutiny of executive implementation.
  • H3 (The Substance-Specificity Disconnect): Executive replies present an illusion of substance through text length, while strategically withholding concrete dates, expenditure figures, and accountability designations.
  • H4 (The Bureaucratic Evasion Clustering Hypothesis): Evasion strategies are not randomly distributed but cluster systematically in high-expenditure capital works and state-owned corporate utilities.
  • H5 (The Accountability Trap): Repeated legislative questioning across successive sessions does not induce institutional learning or executive resolution; instead, unresolved issues become chronic, institutionalized fixtures of the legislative calendar.
Pillar · Methodology & Pipeline

2. Data Architecture, Computational Pipeline, and Validation

To investigate these hypotheses, we constructed an open, end-to-end computational pipeline covering the entire digitized archive of the Meghalaya Legislative Assembly from 2021 onwards. The empirical corpus spans two assemblies: the final years of the 10th Assembly (2021–2022) and the 11th Assembly (2023–2026), comprising 11 distinct sitting sessions and 69 sitting days.

2.1 Data Ingestion and Multi-Reader OCR Architecture

Question metadata was scraped directly from the National e-Vidhan Application (NeVA) digital portal. Signed ministerial reply PDFs—often scanned paper documents featuring complex tabular layouts, bilingual headers, and varied physical degradation—were ingested through a multi-stage consensus OCR architecture:

  • Primary Engine: NVIDIA nemotron-ocr-v1 fine-tuned for administrative and legal Indian scripts, achieving an empirical field accuracy of 0.937.
  • Consensus Voting: A three-reader consensus mechanism (OCR-v1, structured parse, and 11B vision model) resolves ambiguous numerals and date formats.
  • Mechanical Verification Gates: Every extracted reply and submission date was checked against strict validation gates: parseability, chronological ordering, and temporal boundary checks within ±120 days of the official session date.
  • Gutter-Guarded Splitter: Recovered complex multi-column and single-column answer tables, while quarantining corrupt or mixed Q&A pages into auditable review logs.

Rather than relying on non-deterministic generative models that invent composite scores or unvalidated probabilities, the analytical pipeline computes six deterministic, reproducible layers where every single record preserves its evidentiary audit trail:

2.3 Corpus Integrity, Verification, and Methodological Boundaries

The pipeline was subjected to rigorous validation benchmarks:

  • Date Verification: A hand-annotated ground-truth audit of 12 randomly sampled signed PDFs yielded 12/12 exact date matches with 0 confident extraction errors.
  • Seat Resolution: 2,217 of 2,217 questions (100.0%) were successfully attributed to an active member, with 57 unique legislators identified.
  • Scope Boundary (Pre-2021 Exclusion): Sittings prior to 2021 exhibited ministerial reply coverage between 0% and 16% on the portal (with multiple sessions having zero uploaded replies). Consequently, to preserve statistical integrity, the corpus is strictly delimited to 2021 onwards.
Methodological Guardrails — What This Monograph Does Not Claim:
  • No Judgments of Administrative Intent: Coded evasion and boilerplate reflect textual features in the written record; they do not measure unobservable private intentions of civil servants.
  • Conservative Recurrence Matching: Recurrence relies on exact normalized string matching. This deliberately under-merges semantic paraphrases rather than risking false-positive aggregations.
  • Single-Year Fiscal Matching: Budget comparison utilizes the 2025–26 State Budget against multi-year question totals—treated strictly as an indicative scrutiny ratio, not an econometric elasticity.
Empirical Findings · Pillar 1

3. Patterns of Legislative Attention: Concentration, Geography, and Fiscal Disconnect

Where does legislative scrutiny focus in a small hill state? Conventional democratic theory suggests that questions reflect the full spectrum of state administrative responsibilities. The empirical record in Meghalaya reveals the exact opposite: an extreme concentration of floor attention on immediate physical works, profound geographic imbalances, and an acute disconnect between fiscal allocations and legislative scrutiny.

When combined with Power (179 questions) and Public Health Engineering (135 questions), the top five departments account for nearly 43% of total parliamentary scrutiny.

3.4 The Standing Agenda: Sustained Scrutiny Across Sittings

While each session brings novel local controversies, a resilient core of 194 subject families persists across multiple sittings — forming the institutionalized Standing Agenda of the legislature (Figure 5). 'Eklavya Model Residential Schools (EMRS)' recurs most frequently (appearing in 6 of 11 sittings), followed by 'Jal Jeevan Mission' (5 sittings), 'New Shillong Township' (5 sittings), and 'Khasi and Garo Language' (5 sittings). This longitudinal persistence reflects issues where public demand is intense but executive delivery remains chronically unresolved.

3.5 Member Political Economy: The Opposition Monopoly

Who actually drives legislative oversight? The empirical answer is unambiguous: the opposition coalition accounts for 1,768 of 2,217 questions (79.7%) (Figure 15 and Figure 19). In parliamentary systems modeled on Westminster, ruling party backbenchers theoretically share the responsibility of scrutinizing government performance. In Meghalaya, ruling coalition MLAs are virtually silent during Question Hour.

Floor questioning is heavily centralized in a small vanguard of experienced opposition legislators. Dr. Mukul Sangma (AITC, Songsak) asked 230 questions across 24 departments, acting as a one-man shadow cabinet. He is followed by Brightstarwell Marbaniang (VPP, Mawlai), Mayralborn Syiem (UDP/Opposition, Nongpoh), Heaving Stone Kharpran (VPP, Mawryngkneng), and Adelbert Nongrum (VPP, North Shillong). Together, the top five questioners generate over one-third of the legislature's entire inquiry output.

Empirical Findings · Pillar 2

4. Dynamics and Quality of Executive Responsiveness

What happens after a question is tabled? Once the executive receives a legislative demand, what is the character of its response? Through our optical extraction of 2,186 signed ministerial replies, we conduct the first systematic textual audit of ministerial replies in an Indian state assembly. The findings demonstrate that executive responsiveness is characterized by rapid formal delivery coupled with profound evidentiary evasion.

4.1 Demand Typology and Floor Modality

Analyzing the linguistic formulation of legislative questions reveals that 73.5% (1,629 questions) demand raw informational disclosure — requesting statistics, contractor lists, beneficiary counts, and expenditure breakdowns. Only 16.5% (366 questions) demand affirmative executive action, 5.3% seek policy explanations, and 4.7% pursue institutional accountability for prior failures (Figure 6 and Figure 14).

Furthermore, 75.2% of all submissions (1,667) are 'Starred' questions, requiring oral replies on the floor of the House. This overwhelming preference for oral questions highlights the combative, spectacle-driven nature of Question Hour: legislators value the opportunity for live verbal confrontation and supplementary cross-examination over written documentary submissions.

Question volume fluctuates dynamically across political cycles and assembly transitions (Figure 7 and Figure 8). Floor questioning peaked at 353 questions in Eleventh Autumn Session, 2023, marking the high-water mark of opposition assertiveness. 4.5 Anatomy of Bureaucratic Evasion and Self-Contradictions

Using our ported evasion signal registry, we identify four primary evasion strategies deployed by departments:

  1. Procedural Delay ('The Waiting Game'): Utilizing passive formulas like 'matter is under examination', 'information is being collected', or 'awaiting administrative approval' to defer substantive answers.
  2. Dependency Shifting ('Blame Central'): Deflecting accountability onto the Union Government—frequently asserting that state projects are stalled because 'funds are awaited from Ministry of Jal Shakti/DONER/NEC'.
  3. Jurisdictional Deflection ('Not My Purview'): Claiming that issues fall strictly within the constitutional remit of Autonomous District Councils (ADCs) under the Sixth Schedule, thereby absolving state departments.
  4. Indefinite Futures: Promising completion 'at the earliest', 'in due course', or 'as soon as feasible' without fixing calendar milestones.

Identifying a subject that returns in a later sitting requires matching this sitting's wording to the last one. The gates that decide a merge
Five checks run in order, cheapest first, and any of them can stop a merge:
1. Portfolio isolation. Questions are only ever matched inside one department; no merge crosses a department boundary.
2. Locality guard. Conflicting districts or towns block a merge outright — East Jaintia is never folded into West Jaintia. District names are matched longest-first, so 'West Khasi Hills' inside 'South West Khasi Hills' cannot be misread.
3. Named-scheme guard. Two different named programmes are never merged, however similar the sentence around them: a Primary Health Centre and a Community Health Centre share a head noun, which is exactly why a similarity model alone would confuse them.
4. Specificity requirement. A merge needs at least one shared specific word. Sharing only generic ones ('health', 'centre') is not evidence of a shared subject, and a subject naming two programmes is attached to both families instead of bridging them.
5. Verification and provenance. Borderline pairs are put to meta/llama-3.2-11b-vision-instruct; a pair that no gate can decide is not merged — it is written to data/review/semantic_adjudication.csv for a person. Every cluster keeps the variants it merged, the NeVA question numbers behind it, and the reason for each decision.

5.2 In-Depth Qualitative Case Studies

Case Study A: New Shillong Township — Five Sessions of Administrative Inertia
The proposed expansion of New Shillong Township (NST) at Mawdiangdiang has been raised across five consecutive sessions between 2021 and 2026 (Planning, Urban Affairs, and PHE). In each session, legislators raised acute concerns regarding farmer land acquisition compensation, bulk water supply infrastructure, and road connectivity. In September 2022, the government replied that 'land acquisition is under active examination'. In February 2024, the exact same formula was returned: 'land acquisition compensation is being processed by the Deputy Commissioner'. By August 2026, when questioned on the ₹400-crore New Shillong Water Supply Scheme, the executive conceded that work remained contingent upon pending approvals. Five years of sustained parliamentary scrutiny produced zero progression along the project execution lifecycle.
Case Study B: Tura Medical College — The Regression of a Flagship Healthcare Institution
Tura Medical College represents the only subject family in the corpus classified as Regressed. Initially sanctioned in 2018 with a 2021 commissioning target, the project was questioned in four separate sessions. In the Tenth Budget Session (2022), the government assured the House that physical progress stood at 65% and the college would admit students by 2023. However, by the Eleventh Autumn Session (2024), subsequent questioning revealed that the construction contractor had abandoned the site, disputes over revised cost estimates had stalled arbitration, and completion was deferred indefinitely to 2027. Here, legislative questioning did not cause delivery; it merely chronicled the slow institutional decay of a major public hospital.
Case Study C: Khasi and Garo Language Inclusion — From Strategic Deflection to Legislative Action
In contrast, the campaign for the inclusion of Khasi and Garo languages in the Eighth Schedule of the Constitution illustrates how persistent opposition scrutiny can eventually force executive policy shifts. Between March 2021 and February 2026, Chief Minister Conrad Sangma returned identical one-sentence deflections: 'Sir, the matter is pending with the Govt. of India.' Faced with mounting cross-party pressure and public mobilizations raised in every single session, the executive finally broke the cycle in August 2026, moving beyond passive deflection to enact the Meghalaya Official Languages Ordinance, 2026, thereby officially establishing both languages at the state level.
Case Study D: MeECL, Load Shedding, and the Saubhagya Debacle
The Power Department and its distribution arm (MePDCL) represent the focal point of opposition accusations concerning corruption, blackouts, and financial insolvency. Questions regarding the Saubhagya Rural Electrification Scheme were raised in three sessions, probing allegations of inflated procurement costs and uninstalled meters. The executive systematically resisted disclosing complete contract lists, repeatedly answering that 'reply is as per Statement placed on the table of the House' while deflecting responsibility onto central inspecting agencies. Meanwhile, recurring questions on chronic 4-to-6-hour load shedding in the Garo Hills plain belt received non-committal replies asserting that outages were due to 'unprecedented water level depletion at Umiam reservoir', concealing deep structural defaults in central power purchases.
Case Study E: Successful Resolution — EMRS and Jal Jeevan Mission Connections
What characterizes the 14 subject families that reached successful resolution? In both Eklavya Model Residential Schools (EMRS) (Education) and Jal Jeevan Mission (JJM) (PHE), legislative questions focused on highly quantifiable, centrally tracked schemes with standardized physical metrics. In EMRS (raised in 6 sessions), MLAs tracked specific sanction orders, leading the Minister of Education in August 2026 to confirm 24 fully sanctioned schools with 52 sanctioned posts each. In JJM, scrutiny forced the department to report verified household tap connections climbing from 83,000 in 2021 to 5,47,670 by August 2026. Resolution occurs when legislative demands align with high-visibility, centrally audited quantitative targets where civil servants cannot hide behind vague prose.

Policy Synthesis · Institutional Reform

7. Discussion and Institutional Recommendations: Dismantling the Paper Leviathan

The empirical findings of this monograph illuminate what we define as the 'Paper Leviathan' Paradox in Indian subnational governance. The state bureaucracy demonstrates exquisite formal compliance: question files are logged, answers are routed through ministerial secretariats, and signed PDFs are uploaded to the NeVA portal within an impressive 7-day median window (98.6% compliance). Yet behind this facade of digital efficiency lies profound substantive immunity: 98.0% of replies avoid binding completion dates, 69.9% evidence zero delivery stages, and 62.1% of recurring governance failures remain permanently unresolved.

The Institutional Mechanism: Subnational Question Hour functions as a pressure-release valve. Because parliamentary rules lack binding mechanisms to verify whether written ministerial assurances are ever executed, departments treat Question Hour as a communication hurdle to be cleared with vague rhetoric, rather than a managerial driver of delivery.

7.1 Five Actionable Reforms for State Legislative Assemblies

To bridge the Preparedness Gap and restore parliamentary oversight as an engine of real-world accountability, we propose five structural reforms:

  1. Statutory Codification of Legislative Assurances: Transform the Committee on Government Assurances into an automated, digital tracking organ. Any reply containing assurance signals or promising action 'in due course' must be automatically tagged with a statutory 90-day clock, requiring the department to table an Action-Taken Report (ATR) in the subsequent session.
  2. Evidence-Mandated Reply Templates: Replace free-form prose replies with standardized, machine-readable answer templates. For all capital works and public procurement inquiries, the template must mandate: (a) Sanctioned Amount, (b) Cumulative Expenditure, (c) Physical Progress Percentage, (d) Contractor Name & Award Date, (e) Binding Completion Target, and (f) Designated Executive Engineer / Accountable Officer.
  3. Institutionalized Pre-Session Scrutiny Dossiers: Prior to each assembly session, the Assembly Secretariat and planning agencies should equip department heads with automated intelligence dossiers (derived from the Layer 5 and Layer 6 pipelines) summarizing unresolved historical questions, recurring vulnerabilities, and pending commitments.
  4. Public NeVA Analytics Dashboard: Demystify assembly records for civil society, investigative journalists, and citizens. NeVA should publish live, interactive visual analytics displaying departmental reply quality, contradiction flags, and longitudinal resolution tracking, transforming closed legislative files into public accountability tools.
  5. Strengthening Floor Follow-Up on Unstarred Questions: Expand floor rights for Unstarred (written) questions. When written replies fail to meet minimum evidentiary specificity or contradict prior submissions, the Speaker should permit a dedicated 30-minute 'Accountability Call' allowing members to challenge defective answers before the House.
